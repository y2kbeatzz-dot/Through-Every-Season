01 / Understanding

Start with compassion.

People deserve to be heard without judgment. Talking about self-harm or suicidal thoughts can be difficult; asking for support is a meaningful first step.

01

Self-harm deserves care

Self-harm can be a response to emotional distress. A health professional can help you understand what is happening and explore support.

02

Thoughts can be talked about

You can tell someone about suicidal thoughts, even if you are unsure how to explain them. Start with one honest sentence: “I'm struggling and I need support.”

03

Support can change things

Self-harm and suicidal intent are not the same, but both need attention. You deserve help whether this is the first time or something you have lived with for a while.

02 / This moment

One small step
is enough to start.

Choose something that fits right now. These are small coping ideas, alongside reaching people and professional support.

You do not have to explain everything to ask someone to stay with you.

Notice the present

Find three things you can see.

Rest your feet on the floor if comfortable. Name three things around you, listen for two sounds, and notice one neutral sensation. Take it slowly; you can stop if it does not help.

I need more support

If you might act on suicidal thoughts, have a serious injury, or have taken something harmful, seek urgent help now.

03 / Being there

You can be a
person who listens.

You do not need perfect words. Take someone seriously, stay present, and help them connect with support.

Ask directly, with kindness

Try: “I've noticed you're having a hard time. Are you thinking about suicide?” Listen without arguing, judging, or rushing to solve everything. Let them know you care.

Take safety seriously

If someone is in immediate danger, contact emergency services. If it is safe for you, stay with them or help another trusted person be with them while help is arranged. Do not promise to keep a safety concern secret.

Help them reach support

Offer to sit with them while they contact a counselor or trusted person. You can also contact 988 in the United States for guidance about supporting someone.

Keep showing up

A simple check-in later can matter. Ask what would be useful, respect their pace, and remember that you can seek support for yourself too.

04 / Seasons & celebrations

There is no required
way to feel.

Holidays can bring comfort, loneliness, grief, or a mix of feelings. You can celebrate differently, leave early, keep a familiar routine, or reach someone you trust.

Make a smaller plan

Choose a manageable part of the day. Give yourself a quiet break or plan a check-in with someone supportive.

Set a gentle boundary

“I can stay for a little while.” “I'd rather skip that conversation.” You can decide what feels manageable for you.

Leave room for grief

You do not have to turn loss into a celebration. A familiar ritual, a conversation, or time with a trusted person may feel right.

05 / Reach out

Someone can listen.

In the United States, call or text 988, or chat with the 988 Lifeline. It is available 24/7 for emotional distress and suicidal thoughts.

Immediate danger or serious injury? Call 911 in the US or your local emergency number. If you have already harmed yourself, seek medical care.
